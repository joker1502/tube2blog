You converted a 20-minute video, and the draft came back at 1,400 words. Is that enough? Too much? If you've ever stared at a word count wondering whether to pad or cut, you already know the real problem: most advice online says "longer is better," which is wrong, and the rest says "write for the reader," which is useless without numbers.

Post length matters — but not for the reason you think. Google doesn't rank pages because they're long; it ranks pages that fully answer a query. Length is just a side effect of coverage. So the honest question isn't "how many words?" It's "how many words does *this* video's content justify?" Here's how to answer it for every video you repurpose.

## Why Word Count Matters (Only as a Signal, Not a Target)

Treat any single number as a target and you'll make the classic mistake: you'll stretch a 5-minute tip video into 1,800 words of filler, or you'll cut a 40-minute deep dive into 600 words that leave every question half-answered. Both fail for the same reason — the post is no longer sized to the content.

Length matters in three specific ways:

- **Ranking thresholds.** In most niches, posts under 800 words rarely earn featured snippets or top-10 placement for competitive queries. Posts between 1,200 and 2,500 words are where informational content typically starts ranking consistently.
- **Reader retention.** A post that's longer than its substance delivers reads slowly and drops completion rates. A post that's shorter than its topic feels thin and untrustworthy.
- **Internal linking surface.** Every section heading is a place where a natural internal link fits. Longer posts — when justified — give you more of these.

The practical takeaway: use word count as a *check*, not a *goal*. Write the post to cover the video's actual substance, then confirm the result lands in the right range for your format. If it's way under, you're leaving value on the table. If it's way over, you're padding.

## Match Word Count to Video Type: The Benchmark Table

Different video formats carry different amounts of transferable substance. A tutorial's value is in its steps; a vlog's value is mostly in its personality, which doesn't survive the jump to text. The table below is a practical starting range for each format:

| Video type | Typical video length | Recommended post length | What the post should carry over |
|:-----------|:--------------------|:----------------------|:-------------------------------|
| Tutorial / how-to | 5-15 min | 1,200-1,800 words | Steps, setup details, pitfalls, before/after |
| Deep dive / explainer | 15-40 min | 2,000-3,000 words | Concepts, examples, data, edge cases |
| Podcast / interview | 30-60 min | 1,500-2,500 words | Key quotes, insights, summaries per segment |
| Product review | 5-15 min | 1,000-1,600 words | Criteria, testing method, verdict, alternatives |
| Shorts / vertical clips | 30-60 sec | 500-800 words | The single tip, expanded with one example |
| Vlog / lifestyle | 10-20 min | 600-1,000 words | Story beats, takeaways — cut the rest |

Two rules of thumb that beat any single number: (1) if the video has no more than three distinct ideas, a 600-900 word post is correct — don't inflate it; (2) if the video covers a process with five or more steps, you're likely underwriting at anything below 1,200 words, because each step needs its own explanation.

## A Simple Formula for Sizing Any Video's Post

You don't need to guess. Run the video through this three-step formula in about two minutes:

**Step 1: Count the transferable ideas.** Watch the video once (or skim its chapter list) and count the points that would still make sense to someone who never watches it. Not the jokes, not the rambling — the actual content points. A typical tutorial has 5-8. A deep dive has 8-15.

**Step 2: Multiply by 150-200 words per idea.** Each idea needs at least one paragraph: the point, a concrete example, and a mini-explanation. That's roughly 150-200 words each. So a 6-idea tutorial lands at 900-1,200 words before you add the intro, comparison section, and summary — which pushes the finished post to the 1,200-1,800 range in the table above.

**Step 3: Add the structural overhead.** Intro (100-150 words), comparison or table section (200-300 words), and summary (80-120 words). Add those to your idea total and you have your target range. If your draft falls outside it by more than 30%, you've either missed content the video had or added content the video never supported — fix the coverage, not the word count.

## Long-Form vs. Tight Post: Which Performs Better?

| Factor | Tight post (500-900 words) | Long-form post (1,800+ words) |
|:-------|:---------------------------|:------------------------------|
| Best for | Shorts, quick tips, single-topic videos | Tutorials, deep dives, comprehensive guides |
| Ranking ceiling | Low for competitive queries | High when substance justifies length |
| Read completion | High — readers finish quickly | High only if structure is scannable |
| AI content risk | Low — less room for filler | High — padding is easy to spot |
| Update frequency | Rarely needs updates | Needs periodic refreshes |

Long-form only wins when the video actually contains the depth. A 45-minute webinar repurposed as a 600-word post is a waste; a 60-second Short repurposed as a 2,500-word post is filler. Both are the same mistake: ignoring the source material's real density.

The good news is that you don't have to decide this from scratch every time. If you follow a proper [transcript-to-blog workflow](/blog/youtube-transcript-to-blog-workflow), the draft itself reveals the right size — a thin transcript produces a thin draft, and a dense one produces a dense one. Your job is to verify the draft matches the video's actual substance, then size accordingly.

## Summary

There's no universal word count for YouTube-to-blog posts — but there is a reliable method: count the video's transferable ideas, multiply by 150-200 words each, add intro, comparison, and summary overhead, and check the result against the per-format ranges in the table above. Use length as a coverage check, never as a filler target. A tight post that answers the query beats a padded post that buries it — every time.
